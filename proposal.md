# An AI Workforce Adjustment Compact for Australia

**Policy client and process:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workforce, Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR), advising implementation of the *National AI Plan* following DEWR's 2026 *AI and employment in Australia* monitoring report.

## Executive summary

Australia does not yet face economy-wide AI unemployment. DEWR (2026) finds that employment in the fifth of occupations most exposed to AI automation grew by 5.6% between November 2022 and February 2026, compared with 9.5% in the least-exposed fifth. Its model detects a modest negative association, not causal proof of AI job loss. Policy should remain proportionate and evidence-triggered.

This brief recommends a two-year **AI Workforce Adjustment Compact**. Government would replace part of the wages of at-risk employees while employers fund accredited training and retain or redeploy participants. A complementary workplace AI consultation standard would give workers information and review rights. The $247 million pilot would target demonstrated task change rather than predicted occupational extinction. Quarterly monitoring, matched comparison groups and explicit continuation thresholds would protect public funds while helping productivity gains translate into secure work and higher-value tasks.

## Problem definition and policy context

AI changes labour demand at the task level. Under Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2019) framework, automation transfers tasks from labour to capital, reducing labour demand through a **displacement effect**. Higher productivity can lower costs, increase output and create complementary work, while newly created tasks produce a **reinstatement effect**. Jobs therefore rarely disappear as indivisible units. Outcomes depend on which tasks change, the productivity gained and whether workers can move into new tasks.

Exposure is not displacement. Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA 2025) concludes that generative AI is more likely to augment jobs than replace them, although impacts vary across occupations and groups. DEWR's latest evidence shows a resilient aggregate labour market in February 2026: unemployment was 4.2%, the employment-to-population ratio was 64.0% and underutilisation was below its pre-COVID average. However, slower employment, hours and vacancy growth in highly exposed occupations provides a credible early-warning signal (DEWR 2026).

The current policy mix does not fully insure this transition. The *National AI Plan* offers one million subsidised places in a short AI microskill course and supports broad digital literacy (DISR 2025a; Ayres and Rishworth 2025). These measures help workers use AI, but they do not replace earnings during substantial retraining, require employer redeployment or give workers meaningful information about workplace systems.

## Workforce impacts

AI can augment judgement-intensive work while substituting for codifiable cognitive tasks. The Productivity Commission (2024) identifies particular productivity scope in services, which account for about 80% of Australian GDP and 90% of employment. Yet a firm can introduce a “so-so” technology that saves labour without generating enough output or new tasks to offset displacement.

Employment effects will consequently differ within occupations. Clerical workers may lose data-entry and document-processing tasks while retaining exception handling and client contact. Professionals may complete research and drafting faster, but junior workers risk losing the routine tasks through which they develop judgement. If AI complements scarce expertise, productivity and wages can rise. If it substitutes for routine work while employers hold bargaining power, gains can accrue to profits while wages, hours or entry-level hiring weaken.

Routine cognitive occupations have the highest average automation exposure, and clerical and administrative workers have shown relative softening (DEWR 2026). Women, mature-age workers, people with disability, First Nations people and regional workers may face additional barriers. Only 29% of regional organisations were adopting AI compared with 40% of metropolitan organisations, while one in five Australians remained digitally excluded (DISR 2025b). A universal course offer cannot remove lost-income or employer-coordination barriers.

## Policy options

Evaluation uses four equally weighted criteria: **efficiency** (improved matching and productivity), **equity** (support reaches workers bearing adjustment costs), **feasibility** (administrative and political deliverability), and **fiscal discipline** (low deadweight loss and measurable value).

| Option and mechanism | Target and indicative annual public cost | Efficiency | Equity | Feasibility | Fiscal discipline |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Portable AI Transition Accounts:** a $3,000 credit for accredited training chosen by the worker | 50,000 workers in high-exposure occupations; **$165m** including $15m administration | Medium: supports mobility but course-job matching may be weak | Medium: portable, but workers with time and information benefit most | High: can use existing training providers | Medium-low: high risk of paying for training that would occur anyway |
| **2. Employer-linked train-before-redundancy pilot:** government replaces 60% of wages for up to 200 training hours; employers pay course fees, the remaining wage and retain or redeploy participants for six months | 20,000 employees where verified AI adoption materially changes tasks; **$106m** including $10m administration | High: preserves productive worker-firm matches and ties training to changed tasks | High: removes the lost-income barrier; employer contribution shares costs | Medium: requires evidence, accredited plans and compliance checks | High: co-funding and retention reduce deadweight loss |
| **3. Workplace AI consultation and review standard:** require disclosure of affected tasks and data, consultation before material deployment, human review of consequential automated decisions, and a documented redeployment assessment | Workers subject to workplace AI; **$25m establishment cost** for guidance, enforcement capability and small-business support | Medium-high: worker knowledge can improve system design and trust | High: protects voice, privacy and procedural fairness | Medium: requires Fair Work and work-health-and-safety coordination | High: modest budget, though employers bear compliance costs |

Option 1 offers choice and scale, but duplicates broad training and places the information burden on individuals. Option 3 corrects information asymmetry, but consultation cannot finance adjustment. Option 2 performs best because it targets lost wages, employer coordination and task-specific training. Eligibility rules and evaluation must limit subsidies for training that would occur anyway.

## Recommended package and implementation

DEWR should combine **Options 2 and 3** in a two-year pilot. The public cost is **$247 million**: $212 million for two annual training cohorts, $25 million to establish the consultation and review standard, and $10 million for data linkage and independent evaluation. These are transparent policy estimates based on 20,000 participants a year, 200 hours, an assumed $40 hourly wage and 60% public replacement. Treasury should confirm behavioural responses and final costings before appropriation.

| Timing | Responsibility and action | Measurable indicators |
|---|---|---|
| **0–6 months** | DEWR and JSA identify eligible occupations using exposure, employment, hours and vacancy trends. Jobs and Skills Councils approve industry training plans. The Fair Work Ombudsman and Safe Work Australia publish consultation guidance. | Baseline earnings, retention, task mix and regional/demographic participation established |
| **6–18 months** | DEWR opens competitive employer applications. Employers document the AI-related task change, consult workers, fund accredited training and commit to six-month retention or redeployment. | 80% course completion; at least 40% participation by women and 30% from regional or other priority groups; 75% retained or redeployed after six months |
| **18–30 months** | An independent evaluator compares participants with matched eligible non-participants. DEWR reports quarterly by occupation and demographic group. | Twelve-month employment and earnings; movement into complementary tasks; productivity per hour; complaints and review outcomes; cost per sustained placement |

Continuation should require a statistically credible improvement in twelve-month employment or earnings and a cost per sustained placement below comparable employment services. If AI-exposed employment deteriorates sharply across multiple DEWR indicators, government can expand places. If no benefit appears, it should end the wage subsidy while retaining useful monitoring and review rights.

## Risks and mitigations

Employers may relabel ordinary training as AI adjustment. Requiring evidence of changed tasks, employer co-payment and repayment after avoidable early dismissal limits gaming. Large firms may capture places, so 30% should be reserved for small and medium enterprises through Jobs and Skills Councils. Training may preserve declining roles rather than mobility; portable credentials and redeployment plans reduce lock-in. Finally, exposure measures can misidentify risk. Quarterly DEWR data and a scheduled twelve-month eligibility review keep the scheme adaptive rather than locking policy to uncertain forecasts.
