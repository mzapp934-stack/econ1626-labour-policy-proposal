An AI Workforce Adjustment Compact for Australia

Policy client and process: Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workforce, Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR), advising implementation of the National AI Plan following DEWR's 2026 AI and employment in Australia monitoring report.

Executive summary

A new report by the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations reveals that between November 2022 and February 2026 employment in the occupations most exposed to automation grew by 5.6%, compared to 9.5% in the least exposed occupations. The department’s model finds a modest negative association but not causal proof of AI job loss. As a result, the department recommends a policy that is proportionate and evidence-triggered.

This briefing proposes a two-year scheme, an AI workforce adjustment compact. The government would replace part of the wages of workers whose jobs are under threat, while the companies involved would fund accredited training and keep or redeploy the participants. A complementary workplace AI consultation standard would give workers information and review rights. Quarterly evaluations, matched comparison groups and pre-defined continuation thresholds would protect public funds and help productivity improvements to be transformed into secure employment and higher-value tasks.

Problem definition and policy context

Artificial intelligence transforms the demand for labour at the level of the task. According to the framework of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), automation transfers tasks from labour to capital and reduces labour demand through the displacement effect. But higher productivity may reduce costs, increase production, and create additional work, which will lead to the reinstatement effect. This means that jobs are rarely wiped out indiscriminately; it will depend on the tasks affected, the productivity gained, and the opportunity to move into new tasks.

Jobs and Skills Australia’s (JSA 2025) survey concluded that generative AI will augment jobs rather than replace them. The labour market as a whole is in robust shape: the unemployment rate was 4.2%, the employment-to-population ratio was 64.0%, and the rate of underutilisation was lower than it had been before COVID. However, DEWR’s evidence indicates a marked slowdown in employment growth, hours worked, and job vacancies in the occupations most at risk (as DEWR 2026 found).

The National AI Plan offers a million subsidised places on a short AI microskill course, and supports broad digital literacy (DISR 2025a; Ayres and Rishworth 2025). These measures will help workers to learn how to use AI, but they do not compensate for the loss of income while training is in progress, nor do they require employers to adapt their management systems, nor are they able to give meaningful information about workplace AI systems. The current policy mix cannot ensure this transition.

Workforce impacts

The Productivity Commission (2024) has identified a large potential for automation and labour savings in the services sector, which represents about 80% of Australian GDP and about 90% of employment. Artificial intelligence can complement a task which is heavily dependent on judgement, and substitute for a codifiable cognitive task. But a firm can introduce a so-so automation, which makes labour less costly without generating enough output or new work to offset the displaced workers.

Artificial intelligence also has different effects within the same occupation. Clerical workers may lose tasks such as data entry and document processing, but they may keep exceptions and customer contact. In the professions, it may speed up research and drafting, but the tasks that train the judgement of junior employees may be eliminated. If artificial intelligence adds to a scarce expertise, productivity and wages can rise; if it replaces routine labour while the employers hold bargaining power, profits rise at the expense of wages, hours, and recruitment.

Routine clerical and administrative positions have the highest exposure to automation, while the exposure in clerical and administrative work has softened (DEWR 2026). Women, mature-age workers, people with disability, First Nations people, and regional workers face extra obstacles. Only 29% of regional organisations are using artificial intelligence compared with 40% of metropolitan organisations. One in five Australians is digitally excluded (DISR 2025b).

Policy options

The evaluation was made on the basis of four criteria which carried equal weight: efficiency (improved matching and productivity), equity (support reaching those undergoing adjustment costs), feasibility (administrative and political deliverability), and fiscal discipline (low deadweight loss and measurable value).

Option 1 offers choice and scale, but also duplicates broad training and places the information burden on individuals. Option 3 corrects information asymmetry, but consultation cannot finance adjustment. The best option is option 2, which focuses on lost income, employer involvement, and training to specific tasks. Eligibility rules and evaluation should exclude subsidizing training that would have happened anyway.

Recommended package and implementation

The cost of the pilot is $247 million: $212 million for two annual cohorts, $25 million for consultation and review, and $10 million for data linkage and independent evaluation. These figures are based on 20,000 participants a year, 200 hours, an assumed $40 per hour wage, and 60% public coverage. Treasury should confirm the costs and benefits before any appropriation.

Continuation should require a statistically credible improvement in twelve-month employment or earnings and a cost per sustained placement below comparable employment services. If AI-exposed employment deteriorates sharply across multiple DEWR indicators, the program can expand. If no benefit appears, it should end the wage subsidy while retaining useful monitoring and review rights.

Risks and mitigations

Risk indicators may not always be accurate. Training can be used to stabilise declining positions rather than promote mobility, and portable credentials and redeployment plans can reduce lock-in. Large companies can monopolise places, so at least 30% of places should be reserved for small and medium enterprises through Jobs and Skills Councils. Quarterly labour-market data from DEWR and a scheduled twelve-month eligibility review keep the scheme adaptive rather than relying on uncertain forecasts. Requiring evidence of changed tasks, employer co-payment and repayment after avoidable early dismissal limits gaming. Portable credentials and redeployment plans reduce lock-in.
